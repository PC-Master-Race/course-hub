Why a Map Matters  
You have now used image AI and audio AI hands -on. Before this course, you may have thought 
of AI as one big thing -- ChatGPT, or maybe just the technology in general. But AI at work is not 
one tool. It is a landscape of different capabilities, each designed for a different type of task.  
  
Understanding the five categories does three things for you:  
  
• It helps you match the right tool to the right task instead of forcing everything through 
one tool that may not be the best fit.  
• It helps you evaluate new tools intelligently so when someone shows you a new AI 
product, you can immediately ask: which category does this belong to?  
• It sets up your capstone directly. The AI Workplace Framework you are building requires 
you to identify which categories apply to your field -- this module gives you the vocabulary to 
do that precisely.

the workplace, and they are not the same thing.

Bonus -- AI Music & Audio Generation for Work  
Transcription is one side of audio AI. The other side is creation: generating original music from a 
text prompt -- no instruments, no studio, no royalty fees. This matters any time you post video 
content for your business or create marketing materials and need background music without 
risking a copyright strike.  
  
Why This Matters for Your Business  
If you have ever posted a Reel, a property walkthrough video, a classroom tutorial, or a 
promo clip -- and added music from Spotify or YouTube -- you may have gotten flagged, 
muted, or had your video taken down. That is copyright law working exactly as intended.  
AI-generated music is different. Because the AI created it from scratch, there is no original 
artist to claim ownership. You can use it in your videos, posts, and client presentations -- 
without worrying about a takedown or a licensing fee  as long as you follow the sites 
licensing rules which normally includes a subscription .

Reflection -- Three Questions to Answer in Writing  
After your image generates, answer each question below in 1 -2 sentences. Be specific and 
honest. These are not graded for the right answer -- they are graded for thoughtfulness.  
  
Question 1 -- Time and Effort 
Would this save you time compared to doing it manually?  
Think about the alternative: hiring a graphic designer, building it in PowerPoint yourself, 
searching stock photo sites, or simply going without the visual. Be specific -- how much time 
does this replace, and how often would you need it?  
  
Question 2 -- Quality and Usability 
Is the output accurate and professional enough to use as -is?  
Look at the image with your professional eye. Would you actually post this, share it with a 
client, or use it in a lesson? If not -- what is wrong with it? Be honest. An image that looks 
almost right but contains errors (wrong number of objects, strange proportions, text that is 
garbled) is not usable -- and noticing that is the skill.  
  
Question 3 -- Prompt Improvement 
What would you change in your prompt to get a better result?  
This is the most important question. Every AI image generation cycle teaches you 
something about how the tool interprets language. What did the AI misunderstand or miss? 
What detail would you add, remove, or clarify? Write one specific change you would make to 
the prompt if you ran it again.  
  
Pro Tip -- Iterating on a Prompt 
If your first result is not what you wanted, do not start over from scratch. Keep what worked 
and fix what did not. Add one specific detail at a time -- changing everything at once makes 
it impossible to know what improved the output.

Two ways AI tools work -- and why the difference matters in your job  
 
You have been learning how to use AI tools since Week 1. You know what a large language 
model is. You know how to write a good prompt. Now let us talk about something that confuses 
a lot of people -- even people who use AI every day.  
Here is the question: what is the difference between a chatbot and an AI agent?  
The old answer used to be: 'Chatbots just follow rules. Agents can think.' That answer is no 
longer good enough. Today, many chatbots -- like the ones you see on company websites -- are 
powered by the same AI as ChatGPT. They can understand your question even if you say it in 
an unusual way. 
So what is the real difference? It comes down to two things: scope and autonomy.  
Scope means: what is the tool allowed to do? Autonomy means: can the tool do things on its 
own, without you telling it every step?

The Simple Version to Remember  
A scoped AI tool -- whether it uses rules or a real language model -- stays inside a defined 
area and waits for you to start the conversation. An autonomous agent gets a goal from you 
and then figures out the steps on its own. The key question to ask about any AI tool is not 'is 
it smart?' It is: 'How much can it do by itself, and what can it reach?'  
 
 
Part 2: Scoped AI -- Helpful, But It Stays in Its Lane  
Have you ever used a chat box on a company website to ask a question? Maybe it helped you 
track a package or find store hours. But when you asked something a little different -- something 
it was not set up for -- it said 'I don't understand' or sent you to a human?  
That is a scoped AI. It is set up to handle a specific set of topics. It will not go outside that area, 
even if it is powered by a smart AI model underneath.  
Important: many of these tools today use real AI -- the same kind as ChatGPT. They can 
understand how you talk and give natural answers. But they are still limited to one topic or 
product on purpose. That limit is a design choice -- not a sign that the tool is weak.  
Definition: Scoped AI  
A scoped AI tool stays inside a set area -- a topic, a product, a company's rules. It waits for 
you to start the conversation. It answers your question. Then it stops. It does not go look for 
other problems to solve. It does not connect to other tools on its own. Modern examples: 
Intercom, Zendesk AI, and bots built on Playlab.ai.

Part 3: Autonomous Agents -- You Give the Goal, It Does the Work  
Think about a really capable coworker. You walk up to them on Monday and say: 'Can you find 
the three best vendors for our supply order, compare their prices, write up a one -page 
summary, and email it to the team by noon?' Then you walk away.

A good coworker can do that. They will figure out the steps themselves. They will use whatever

An AI agent works the same way. You give it a goal. It plans the steps. It uses tools. It finishes

This is not a future idea -- it is happening right now. Microsoft Copilot, tools built on OpenAI's

Definition: Autonomous Agent  
An autonomous agent is an AI tool that receives a goal from you and then plans and 
completes the steps on its own. It can use multiple tools -- like search, email, or a document 
editor -- all in one task. It can also adjust its plan if something goes wrong. You review the 
final result, not every single step.

Part 5: More Power Means More Responsibility  
Here is the important thing to understand: the more an AI tool can do on its own, the more 
carefully you need to watch it.  
A scoped AI that sends the wrong message does it once. You can see it. You can fix it. An 
autonomous agent might send emails, update records, and trigger other actions before you ever 
look at what it did. A small mistake early in the process can turn into a bigger problem by the 
end. 
This does not mean agents are bad. It means they require more careful oversight. More power 
needs more attention, not less.
