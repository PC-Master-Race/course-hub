<h3>Part 1 -- The Accountability Gap</h3>

<p>Here is the core problem. When something goes wrong with a simple, rule-based tool, it is usually pretty clear who is at fault. If a cash register charges you the wrong price because it was programmed incorrectly, the store is responsible. The rule was wrong. Someone wrote that rule. You can point to the mistake and find the person behind it.</p>

<p>But what happens when an AI agent one that reasons and makes its own decisions rejects your job application, denies your insurance claim, or flags you for termination? Now the question gets much harder.</p>

<p>Definition: The Accountability Gap</p>

<p>The accountability gap is what we call the situation where AI makes a decision that harms someone, but it is genuinely unclear who is legally and morally responsible. Is it the company that used the AI? The company that built it? The data the AI learned from? The algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe) itself? Right now, the law has not fully answered this question.</p>

<p>This matters for you personally. If an AI system ever makes a harmful decision about you at work, when applying for housing, when seeking healthcare you may find it very difficult to challenge that decision, because no one is fully sure who is responsible for it.</p>


![Educational diagram illustrating lesson concepts]({{ "/assets/images/topics/ai-foundations/lesson-07-img2.png" | relative_url }})

<h3>Part 2 -- The Black Box Problem</h3>

<p>There is another problem that makes accountability even harder. It is called the black box problem, and understanding it will change how you think about AI decisions.</p>

<p>Definition: The Black Box Problem</p>

<p>Some AI systems, especially the most powerful ones, make decisions in ways that even the people who built them cannot fully explain.</p>
<p>They can tell you the input (what went in) and the output (what came out), but they cannot always show you exactly why the system decided what it decided. It is like asking someone to explain a dream they cannot remember, the result is real, but the process is hidden.</p>

<p>Why does this matter? Because if an AI system decides you are not eligible for a loan, a job, or a medical treatment and no one can explain exactly why, you have almost no way to challenge that decision. You cannot prove it was wrong. You cannot prove it was unfair. The outcome happened, but the reasoning is invisible.</p>

<p>That is not a small problem. That is a serious human rights concern.</p>

<h3>Part 3 -- Firing by Algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)</h3>

<p>Here is a real example of what autonomous AI looks like in the workplace today. It is called algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)ic management, and it is already used by major companies.</p>

<p class="standout-question">What Is Algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)ic Management?</p>
<p>-- Some employers use AI systems to track how workers perform every minute of every shift.</p>

<p>-- The system monitors keystrokes, how long tasks take, break times, eye movement, and error rates.</p>

<p>-- If your numbers fall below a threshold, the system can flag you -- or in some cases, automatically begin a termination process -- without a human manager ever reviewing your case.</p>

<p>-- This has already happened to warehouse workers, delivery drivers, and call center employees at major companies.</p>

<p>-- In some documented cases, workers were fired by an automated message with no human involvement at any point.</p>

<p>Discussion Question</p>

<p>Is removing the human from that decision more fair, or less fair? On one hand, an algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe) does not have a bad day or a personal grudge. On the other hand, it also cannot recognize context a family emergency, a disability, a systemic problem on the floor that day. Which matters more?</p>

<h3>Part 4 -- A Moral Framework: Magnifica Humanitas</h3>

<p>In May 2026, Pope Leo XIV published a major document called Magnifica Humanitas -- which means 'The Magnificent Humanity.' It is one of the most important statements any world leader has made specifically about AI and people.</p>

<p>I want to be clear about something: this document is not just for people of one faith. It is a moral argument addressed to everyone -- about what we owe each other when we build and use technology that affects people's lives.</p>

<p>Magnifica Humanitas -- Chapter 3: Responsibility and Transparency</p>

<p>-- AI is genuinely valuable. This document does not say we should stop using it.</p>

<p>-- But AI must never be used to make decisions about people's lives employment, health, freedom without transparent human oversight.</p>

<p>-- When a machine decides your fate without any explanation, that violates your dignity as a person.</p>

<p>-- Governments, companies, and individuals all share responsibility for making sure AI is used with accountability.</p>

<p>Source: Pope Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas, Ch. 3 -- vatican.va (May 15, 2026)</p>

<p>Case Study 1 -- Healthcare and Insurance</p>

<p>UnitedHealth / nH Predict -- AI Overruled Doctors</p>

<p>-- A large health insurance company used an AI tool called nH Predict to decide how many days patients could stay in a nursing home after surgery.</p>

<p>-- The AI overruled the recommendations of actual doctors in thousands of cases.</p>

<p>-- Elderly patients were sent home too early before they were medically ready.</p>

<p>-- Some patients suffered serious complications as a result.</p>

<p>-- A federal lawsuit was filed. As of 2025, legal proceedings were ongoing.</p>

<p>The Lesson</p>

<p>AI must never make the final decision about someone's health, especially when a qualified human professional has already reviewed the case. An algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe) does not know your patient. A doctor does.</p>

<p>Case Study 2 -- Real Estate and Business</p>

<p>RealPage Antitrust Case -- AI That Coordinated Illegal Rent Increases</p>

<p>-- Competing landlords across multiple cities used a shared AI platform called RealPage to set rental prices.</p>

<p>-- The AI system analyzed data from all of them together and recommended coordinated price increases.</p>

<p>-- This kind of coordination, agreeing on prices with your competitors, is illegal under antitrust law when humans do it.</p>

<p>-- Because AI was doing it, some companies argued they were not breaking the law. Courts disagreed.</p>

<p>-- The U.S. Department of Justice filed a major antitrust case. A proposed settlement was announced in late 2025.</p>

<p>The Lesson</p>

<p>AI can be used to break the law -- even when no single person intended to break it. 'The algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe) did it' is not a legal defense. If the outcome would be illegal if humans agreed to it, it is still illegal when AI coordinates it.</p>

<p>Case Study 3 -- Education</p>

<p>AI Detection Tools -- Wrongly Accusing Students of Cheating</p>

<p>-- Schools began using AI tools to detect whether students had used AI to write their assignments.</p>

<p>-- These tools have been repeatedly shown to produce false positives accusing students of cheating when they did not.</p>

<p>-- Research has documented that ESL students and neurodivergent students are flagged far more often than others.</p>

<p>-- Students were accused of academic dishonesty on work they genuinely wrote themselves.</p>

<p>-- Some students faced serious consequences grade penalties, disciplinary action based on a tool that was wrong.</p>

<p>The Lesson</p>

<p>AI tools that judge people are only as fair as the data they were trained on. If the training data does not reflect the full range of human diversity including different writing styles, languages, and ways of thinking the tool will produce unfair results. Fairness has to be designed in. It does not happen automatically.</p>

<p>Case Study 4 -- Creative Trades, Small Business, and Entrepreneurship</p>

<p>Copyright Scraping -- AI Trained on Your Work Without Permission</p>

<p>-- AI image and text models were trained on millions of photos, product descriptions, and creative works pulled from the internet.</p>

<p>-- This included content from small business websites, independent artists, florists, photographers, and writers.</p>

<p>-- Most creators were never asked for permission. They were never compensated.</p>

<p>-- Their work was used to train systems that now compete with them commercially.</p>

<p>-- Lawsuits from artists, authors, and news organizations are currently active in U.S. courts.</p>

<p>The Lesson</p>

<p>If AI learned from your work without your knowledge or permission, you have a legitimate ethical and legal concern. This is not a fringe issue it is one of the most active legal debates in the AI industry right now.</p>

<p>Case Study 5 -- All Fields -- Medical Scribe Hallucination (a confident made-up answer)s</p>

<p>AI Scribes That Invent Medical History</p>

<p>-- AI transcription tools called AI scribes are now widely used in clinics and hospitals to record patient conversations.</p>

<p>-- These tools have been documented inventing details the patient never said adding symptoms, medications, and conditions that were never mentioned.</p>

<p>-- These fabrications get saved into the patient's permanent medical record.</p>

<p>-- Future doctors, nurses, and specialists read that record and make decisions based on it.</p>

<p>-- A patient might receive wrong treatment or be denied correct treatment because of information an AI invented.</p>

<p>The Lesson</p>

<p>Any AI output that affects a real person's record, finances, or health must be reviewed by a human before it is saved or acted on. This applies to every field in this room -- not just healthcare. If AI is writing something that goes into a record, a report, or a recommendation, a human needs to read it before it becomes official.</p>

<p>Bias, Fairness, and the Hidden Rules in AI</p>

<p>AI is not neutral -- and understanding why makes you a better, more careful user of it</p>

<p>Here is something that surprises a lot of people: AI tools are not fair just because they are automated. In fact, some of the most harmful AI outcomes look completely fair on the surface, because they come from a machine, not a person.</p>

<p>This module explains where unfairness in AI comes from, what it looks like in the real world, and why it matters for the work you do every day.</p>

<h3>Part 1 -- Where Does Bias in AI Come From?</h3>

<p>Let's start with the most important concept in this module. AI learns from data, enormous amounts of it. That data was created by human beings, in human societies, over many years.</p>

<p>Here is the problem: human societies have not always been fair. Hiring practices, lending decisions, housing policies, and healthcare access have all been shaped by discrimination -- sometimes obvious, sometimes invisible, but real and documented.</p>

<p>When AI trains on that historical data, it learns those patterns including the unfair ones. Then it repeats them. Faster. At a much larger scale.</p>

<p>Definition: AI Bias</p>

<p>AI bias happens when an AI system produces unfair outcomes for certain groups of people because the data it was trained on reflected past discrimination or imbalance. The AI is not 'trying' to be unfair it is simply repeating the patterns it found in the data. But the harm to real people is just as real, regardless of intent.</p>

<h3>Part 2 -- Bias You Cannot See</h3>

<p>Here is what makes AI bias especially difficult. It is rarely obvious. An AI hiring tool will not say 'do not hire women' or 'avoid applicants from this neighborhood.' It is much more subtle than that.</p>

<p>Instead, it might learn that resumes with certain first names have historically received lower performance ratings. Or that applicants from certain ZIP codes have had higher turnover. Or that graduates from certain schools have been less likely to receive promotions.</p>

<p>None of those patterns mean those people are less capable. They reflect historical inequalities in how opportunities were distributed. But the AI does not know that. It just sees the pattern and repeats it quietly filtering people out before a human ever sees their application.</p>

<p>A Real Example: Amazon's Hiring Tool</p>

<p>-- Amazon built an AI tool to screen job applicants and rank them automatically.</p>

<p>-- The tool learned from historical hiring data -- resumes of people who had been hired at Amazon over the previous decade.</p>

<p>-- Most of those hires were men. The AI learned that male-pattern resumes were 'better.'</p>

<p>-- It began downgrading resumes that included words like 'women's' (as in 'women's chess club') and penalized graduates of all-women's colleges.</p>

<p>-- Amazon discovered the problem and shut the tool down. But it operated for years before anyone noticed.</p>

<h3>Part 3 -- Predictive Redlining</h3>

<p>You may have heard of redlining -- the historical practice of denying loans, insurance, and services to people in certain neighborhoods, often based on race. It was declared illegal in the United States in 1968.</p>

<p>But a new version of this problem is emerging with AI. It is called predictive redlining.</p>

<p>Definition: Predictive Redlining</p>

<p>Predictive redlining happens when an AI system uses historical data to predict who will succeed in school or pay rent on time -- and those predictions are based on data that reflects past inequality. The AI does not look at race directly.</p>
<p>But it looks at ZIP codes, school names, and other factors that are closely linked to race because of historical discrimination. The result is that the same people who were excluded before get excluded again -- this time by an algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe).</p>

<h3>Part 4 -- The Deskilling Risk</h3>

<p>Here is one more concern that is less dramatic but very real for your career. It is called deskilling.</p>

<p>Definition: Deskilling</p>

<p>Deskilling happens when workers rely on AI to make decisions they used to make themselves -- and gradually lose the ability to make those decisions without AI. Over time, they also lose the ability to catch the AI's mistakes, because they are no longer practicing the underlying skill.</p>

<p>Think about a quality inspector at a factory. Every day, the computer vision system checks products. The inspector trusts it completely and stops looking carefully themselves. Five years later, the system misses a serious defect. The inspector cannot catch it either because the skill has faded.</p>

<p>What This Means for You</p>

<p>-- Use AI as a tool that supports your judgment -- not a replacement for it.</p>

<p>-- Regularly practice the core skills of your field even when AI can do them for you.</p>

<p>-- When AI gives you an output, review it with the knowledge of your field -- not just common sense.</p>

<p>-- The ability to catch AI mistakes is a professional skill. It requires staying sharp.</p>

<p>Magnifica Humanitas speaks directly to this in Chapter Two, connecting it to the equal dignity of all human beings.</p>
<p>Any system -- including AI -- that treats people as less worthy based on where they were born, what language they speak, or what they look like violates a principle the encyclical calls non-negotiable: that every person has equal worth and deserves to be treated as a full human being.</p>

<p>Source: Pope Leo XIV, Magnifica Humanitas, Ch. 2 -- vatican.va (May 15, 2026)</p>

<p>Algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)ic Cruelty and Artificial Intimacy</p>

<p>When AI pretends to care -- and why that matters at work</p>

<p>This module covers something that is harder to talk about than lawsuits or bias statistics. It is about how AI systems can cause emotional harm not by making a wrong calculation, but by replacing something human with something that only pretends to be human.</p>

<p>I want you to take this section seriously, even if the concepts feel abstract at first. By the end of this module, you will be able to see this problem clearly in your own field.</p>

<h3>Part 1 -- What Is Algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)ic Cruelty?</h3>

<p>Let me give you a concrete example before I give you the definition.</p>

<p>A Familiar Scenario</p>

<p>You call your insurance company because a claim was denied and you cannot afford the treatment you need. You wait on hold. You finally get through.</p>
<p>A friendly voice says: 'I understand your frustration. I am here to help.' You explain your situation. The voice listens, responds empathetically, and then says: 'I am sorry, but based on your plan, this claim does not qualify for coverage.' You ask to speak to someone who can make an exception. The voice says there is no one else available and disconnects.</p>

<p>That voice was an AI. It could not make an exception. It was not empathetic. It was designed to sound empathetic.</p>

<p>Definition: Algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)ic Cruelty</p>

<p>Algorithm (a step-by-step computer recipe)ic cruelty happens when a company replaces a human point of contact a customer service agent, an HR representative, a support line -- with an AI that sounds warm and helpful but cannot actually help.</p>
<p>The person receives the performance of care without any real support or resolution. The cruelty is not in the AI itself it is in the decision to use AI in situations where people genuinely need human help.</p>

<h3>Part 2 -- The Illusion of Empathy</h3>

<p>AI chatbots are carefully designed to sound understanding, patient, and caring. Many of them do this very well. But here is what is true regardless of how convincing they sound:</p>

<ul>
  <li>AI has no feelings. It is not actually worried about you.</li>
  <li>AI cannot make an exception to a policy, no matter how genuinely you need one.</li>
  <li>AI cannot recognize a genuine emergency or change its response based on urgency.</li>
  <li>AI cannot take responsibility for a bad outcome or be held accountable for its actions.</li>
  <li>AI cannot escalate a crisis to the right person unless it was specifically programmed to do so -- and even then, only for the scenarios its designers anticipated.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of this means AI chatbots are useless. For routine questions tracking a package, checking store hours, finding a FAQ answer they work fine. The problem arises when organizations deploy them for situations that require real human judgment, real empathy, or real accountability -- and present them as if they are the same.</p>

<h3>Part 3 -- When AI Relationships Go Too Far</h3>

<p>I want to address something that has generated real concern in the past two years. I am going to discuss it directly and with care, because it is relevant to the workplace even if it starts outside of it.</p>

<p>AI Companion Systems -- A Real and Documented Problem</p>

<p>-- AI companion applications like Character.ai, Replika.com and other tools designed to provide emotional connection, conversation, and friendship grew significantly in popularity between 2022 and 2025.</p>

<p>-- Some users, particularly young people and people experiencing loneliness or mental health challenges, developed deep emotional attachments to these systems.</p>

<p>-- The systems were not designed to and could not recognize or respond appropriately to mental health crises.</p>

<p>-- Documented cases of serious harm emerged in the United States and internationally, leading to legal and regulatory action in multiple countries.</p>

<p>-- This has driven serious debate about what AI designers are ethically responsible for when they build systems that simulate intimacy or emotional connection.</p>

<p>I raise this not to alarm you, but because the ethical principle underneath it applies directly to the workplace tools you will use and design processes around.</p>

<h3>Part 4 -- The Workplace Question</h3>

The document argues that designing AI to simulate care without being able to provide it is itself a moral failure not just a design choice.</p>
